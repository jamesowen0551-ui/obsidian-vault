After speculating earlier this month about the efficacy of the Bank of England's proposed policy implementation regime, I feel obliged to put my money where my mouth is and describe a possible post-floor-system framework for the Federal Reserve. To keep the length manageable, this is just a list of the objectives and components of such a framework along with a few observations on the transition and operations in stressed circumstances. For a more comprehensive discussion, see "How the Federal Reserve got so huge, and why and how it can shrink".

Most of these components could be incorporated individually into any framework; they don't have to be a package.

## Objectives

- **Good interest rate control.**
- **Cheap central bank liquidity** to support financial stability and efficiency.
- **Active interbank market** so that banks manage their own liquidity risk most of the time.
- **Fed monetary policy independence.**
- **Low credit and interest rate risk** to avoid encroaching on tasks that should be left to elected officials. Consistent with the Fed's balance sheet principles.

## Components of the framework

**Target for the federal funds rate.** The FOMC should set a point target rather than a range for the overnight federal funds rate. The Fed switched to a range in December 2008 because it was unsure where the funds rate would settle when they moved to the effective lower bound. A target enhances interest rate control by providing market participants a focal point and precise indication of the Fed's intentions (see Meulendycke (1998) and Hanes (2018)). With money market rates well above zero, there is no longer any benefit to using a range, just cost.

The Fed should target the fed funds rate rather than the repo rate because it can maintain good control of the federal funds rate with a light presence in financial markets, and the rate is well-transmitted into other money market rates. To control the repo rate, which is determined in a massive market and moves around for idiosyncratic reasons, the Fed would need to conduct massive operations and yet have no greater ability to influence economic activity. Activity in the fed funds market will pick up if the Fed adopts this proposed framework just as it did when the Federal Reserve shed Treasuries following WWII.

**Corridor.** Set the target fed funds rate in the middle of a policy corridor created by the primary credit rate as the ceiling and the interest on excess reserves rate as the floor.

The target does not need to be near the IOER rate because, as discussed below, discount window borrowing capacity and voluntary required reserves are both available without restriction to banks for liquidity risk management at low cost. The IOER rate needs to be sufficiently below market rates to provide banks a strong incentive to lend in the federal funds market when they have excess reserves.

To help reduce stigma, the primary credit rate is set at a spread above market rates so that banks will choose voluntarily to only use it as a back-up source of funding, allowing the Fed to eliminate rules about use. However, there is a tradeoff involved in choosing the spread of the primary credit rate over the target federal funds rate. If the spread is too low, some small banks and credit unions will seek to use the discount window as an ongoing source of funding, which the Fed does not want. If the spread is too high, the federal funds rate could be excessively volatile. Moreover, a high spread could also contribute to stigma. When primary credit was designed, the Fed concluded that a spread of 100 basis points was necessary to lead almost all banks of all sizes to refrain from borrowing on an ongoing basis.

As a tentative proposal: Set the corridor 100 basis points wide with the primary credit rate 50 bp above the target and the IOER rate 50 bp below.

**Daily open-market operations.** The Fed will conduct daily open market operations to keep reserve supply near reserve demand. A set-it-and-forget-it implementation regime is a pipe dream.

**Primary credit as liquidity backstop.** The Federal Reserve provides primary credit to eligible banks on a no-questions-asked basis; the loans can have maturities of up to 90 days, prepayable and renewable on request. The Federal Reserve should state that eligibility will only be revoked with 31-days' notice. (The Fed has already committed not to call outstanding term primary credit loans if a bank loses eligibility.) The Federal Reserve and, ideally, the other banking agencies should then recognize the borrowing capacity created by pre-positioned collateral as a source of liquidity and a substitute for reserve balances.

Under these circumstances, unused discount window borrowing capacity is economically equivalent to a reserve balance – both are promises of a Federal Reserve Bank to provide funds on demand in the future. Dry powder at the discount window is just as liquid as cash at the Fed, but less costly for the economy because it doesn't take up bank balance sheet capacity. Because the unused capacity is free, discount window borrowing would be an attractive alternative to voluntary reserve balances. Because banks could devote less of their balance sheet to deposits at Federal Reserve Banks and more of their balance sheet to lending to businesses and households, the arrangement would contribute to higher GDP without compromising bank safety and soundness or financial instability.

**Auctions of term primary credit.** The Federal Reserve conducts weekly auctions of one-month and three-month term primary loans in a manner similar to the Term Auction Facility the Fed operated during the GFC. Unlike the TAF, the Fed would specifically refer to such loans as "primary credit". While the TAF was designed to look and feel different from primary credit to avoid catching the stigma, term primary credit loans would be designed to make primary credit borrowing even more ordinary and ubiquitous, reducing stigma.

The auctions would be for a sizeable amount ($200 billion, say) and would have no minimum bid rate. While the initial auctions would likely clear at rates well below market rates, bank participation would increase over time as banks observe their competitors getting cheap funding, and the auction-clearing rate would rise.

**Redesign the discount window.** Redesigning the discount window is too big an issue to discuss in a few sentences. One potentially promising suggestion by Susan McLaughlin is to get rid of primary and secondary credit and have discount window lending for solvent banks and something else entirely for lending to banks near failure.

**Invest the Treasury General Account in repo.** The volatility of the TGA causes volatility in reserve balances, making it more difficult to conduct monetary policy. The wide swings have also contributed to money market volatility as funds flowing into the TGA from money funds translate into reductions in the supply of repo financing. FRBNY should automatically invest the TGA in repo (that is, reverse repo from the Fed's perspective). Doing so would sterilize the impact of changes in the TGA on reserve balances and match any reductions in the supply of repo financing with an increase in supply.

**Outright portfolio weighted toward bills.** The Fed invests only in Treasury securities, repo, and term primary credit loans. The Treasury portfolio should be overweighted in Treasury bills. The short maturity of the portfolio will allow the Fed to sterilize almost any emergency lending in a crisis, preventing an unwanted increase in reserve balances.

**Voluntary reserve targets.** After each FOMC meeting period, each bank establishes a target for its reserve balances (deposits at a Federal Reserve Bank). Reserves within a small band around the target are compensated close to but slightly below the FOMC's target. Reserves above the VRT are compensated at the deposit rate. If reserves end up below the target, the bank's interest payment is reduced to cost slightly more than making up the shortfall by borrowing at the discount window (so that banks have an incentive to borrow). Potentially use reserve averaging.

The IOVR rate is near the FOMC's target so that the opportunity cost of holding reserve balances for liquidity management purposes is low. The IOVR rate is slightly below the target to balance the tradeoff between the financial stability and efficiency benefits of providing banks low-cost liquidity and the risk to Fed independence from having an unbounded balance sheet. A slightly below-market IOVR rate will provide banks a modest incentive to economize on reserve balances versus other sources of liquidity such as reverse repurchase agreements and, as discussed in the next section, capacity to borrow from the discount window. If reserve balances become too abundant, the fed funds rate will equal the IOVR rate and the FOMC will not hit its target.

The Fed will state clearly that meeting a voluntary reserve target is voluntary apart from the financial consequences. As a result, unlike required reserves, the reserves will qualify as high-quality liquid assets under the Basel standard for the Liquidity Coverage Ratio.

## Transition

- Put the components in place.
- Vigorously train examiners that banks can largely replace reserve balances with discount window capacity.
- Continue QT until the fed funds rate is in line with the FOMC target which is above the IOER rate. The combination of allowing banks to reduce reserve balances and providing them a financial incentive to do so will lead to further gradual reduction in reserves and therefore the size of the Fed.
- Actively control excessive volatility in reserve balances with open-market operations during transition.

## Crises and Zero Lower Bound

- **No more Quantitative Easing.** The purpose of QE is entirely to shift interest rate risk from the public to the Fed, thereby reducing longer-term interest rates. The Fed should instead limit its interest rate risk. If the government wishes to push down long-term interest rates by taking interest rate risk out of public hands, Treasury can buy back longer-term securities and shorten the maturity of its borrowings.
- If large-scale lending operations or asset purchases to support market functioning are necessary in some circumstances, sterilize the consequence for reserve balances.
- If emergency programs cannot be sterilized, set the target federal funds rate equal to the IOER rate and transition temporarily to a floor system.
